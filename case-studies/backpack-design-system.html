Frontline Education — Backpack, the company's internal design system · Product Designer and de facto system owner

Building a design system with no team — then getting one by proving it worked

What I did

Built Frontline's design system from scratch as the only designer on it, drove adoption across the whole design team, then got it into production code through a side door after being denied the resources to build it the normal way.

Why I did it

Frontline had grown by acquisition into roughly 30 products, each with its own patterns and no shared language between any of them. Nothing was reusable, nothing was consistent, and neither design nor engineering could move quickly across products.

What broke

I asked for a dev team to turn the system into real coded components. I got turned down twice. Without a coded library, every developer had to eyeball my Figma file and rebuild each component by hand.

What changed

I found an unrelated engineering team already building their own component library and got them to build it on my specs instead — production code, without the headcount I'd been refused. That made the system's value concrete enough that a new CPO later funded a dedicated team to take it further. 336 components, six products.

Context

When I joined Frontline in September 2022, the company was a portfolio of roughly 30 products acquired over many years, each with its own patterns, conventions, and visual language. There was no shared component library and no common product language across teams. An earlier attempt at a design system, internally called Lunchbox, had stalled and never got adopted.

Leadership had already decided the new system would be based on Frontline Central, one of the company's most established products. My job was to turn that decision into something designers and developers could actually use.

Building the foundation

I built the system's Figma architecture from the ground up: separate libraries for foundations, components, and patterns, audited out of Frontline Central's existing UI. Between September 2022 and March 2023, I documented usage rules, built the variables and styles, and worked with other designers to construct the component set. By May 2023 the whole design team had adopted it. It's now known internally as Backpack, and the asset library holds 336 components.

I also became the de facto component architect and QA reviewer for the rest of the team. Most designers were still building their Figma proficiency, so I reviewed — and often rebuilt — the components other people made, to keep the system internally consistent. That upkeep mattered as much as the original build. A system starts to fragment the moment its own contributors drift from the rules, and with a team still learning the tool, that drift was constant.

Getting turned down for the resources to finish the job

By August 2023, developers outside the design team were requesting Figma access to reference the system directly. Good adoption signal — but it exposed the real gap. Without a coded component library, every dev had to eyeball the Figma file and rebuild each component from scratch. The design system existed; the engineering half of it didn't.

I brought my manager two ways to close that gap: stand up a small team, a PM plus dedicated dev resources, to build and govern a React library, or pull a couple of devs part-time and let me run point as both PM and designer.

Both were rejected. The CPO at the time didn't see enough value to justify moving people around.

Rather than let that stall the system, I built "exploded view" specs for each component — every measurement, spacing value, and state broken out and labeled directly in Figma — so a developer with no library access could reproduce a component accurately instead of guessing. It wasn't a substitute for a real library, but it closed the worst of the gap and kept the system usable while I looked for a way forward.

Finding the opening

In February 2024, a PM mentioned that a group of developers on the People Solutions side — rebuilding Recruiting & Hiring from the ground up — had started planning their own shared component library. I tracked down who owned it, a senior front-end developer, and asked him to walk me through the initiative.

Their plan was to build on Tailwind with minimal customization: essentially unstyled utility components, unconnected to anything the design team was doing. I pitched reskinning their Tailwind components to match Backpack instead. He was hesitant. It meant adding scope to a project that already had a deadline.

So I didn't pitch it in the abstract. I showed him what the Backpack documentation actually did for his team: a clear, always-current reference for which component to use and when — exactly the kind of guidance design was too thin on the ground to give in person on every decision his developers faced. Reskinning their components to match Backpack wasn't so much extra work as a way to take a pile of open questions off his team's plate. He agreed, and engineering built the library straight off my Figma specs.

That's the part of this project I keep coming back to. I never had the authority to make anyone adopt Backpack. It spread because, for the one team I needed, using it turned out to be less work than not using it.

Outcome

The React library gave developers what the exploded-view specs never could: components they could pull from directly instead of rebuilding by hand. The library also spread further than it was scoped to. It was originally meant for just two products — the new Recruiting & Hiring and K-12 Job Spot — but once it was built on Backpack instead of unstyled Tailwind, four more products picked it up, bringing the total to six.

When a new CPO joined and made design-system investment a priority, he set aside dedicated headcount — a new PM, a new dev team, and another designer — now kicking off a full visual overhaul and rollout on the foundation I'd already put in place. The resourcing ask that got rejected in 2023 was effectively granted in 2024, once the value was in front of people instead of hypothetical.

What I'd do differently

If I were starting over, I'd begin with a mature third-party component library rather than building Backpack from scratch. Most of the cost here wasn't design judgment — it was the overhead of building every component from zero, writing usage documentation with no reference point, and asking engineering to commit to a library architecture with no prior art to lean on. Starting from something proven would have freed up the bulk of my time for adoption and governance, which is where the actual bottleneck turned out to be.

What this project taught me

The biggest thing I took from it: a design system is an organizational problem more than a design one. Getting the components right is the entry fee, not the win. What decides whether a system survives is everything around it — whether leadership backs adoption, whether governance exists, whether teams have the capacity to migrate, and whether they can see what it does for their business. When those conditions are in place, the work is straightforward. When they aren't, you end up building them yourself, one team at a time, until the value is too obvious to ignore. That second version is the one I lived.
